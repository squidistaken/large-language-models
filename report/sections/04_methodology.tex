\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

We used one primary model for all prompting and fine-tuning conditions, keeping the choice of model out of the comparisons, and a second model for prompting only.

\subsection{Models}
\label{sec:methodology_models}

We chose both models for their size, multilingual coverage, licence and accessibility: both are open-weight, released under the Apache~2.0 licence on the Hugging Face Hub, and fit on a single NVIDIA A100 (40~GB) of the university's high-performance computing cluster. All experiments used PyTorch~2.14.0, Transformers~5.17.0 and PEFT~0.21.2. The primary model is EuroLLM-9B-Instruct \parencite{martins2025eurollm}, hereafter EuroLLM-9B (\path{utter-project/EuroLLM-9B-Instruct}, revision \texttt{f7ae2bc3}). It has 9.15B parameters and was pretrained on about four trillion tokens in 35 languages, including all official EU languages, so Croatian is one of the languages it was built for. Prompting and fine-tuning used the same 4-bit version of it, leaving the way the model is adapted as the only difference between the two approaches. The second model is Qwen3.5-9B \parencite{qwen2026qwen35} (\path{Qwen/Qwen3.5-9B}, revision \texttt{c2022362}), a widely used open-weight model of the same size; its model card claims support for ``201 languages and dialects'' but does not list them, so we do not know whether Croatian was deliberately covered. As it was never fine-tuned, it had no 4-bit runs to match, and we ran it at its default bfloat16 (bf16) precision, which fits on the A100. The two models were thus prompted at different precisions. We disabled its reasoning mode to make it answer with the label list directly.

\subsection{Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning}
\label{sec:methodology_peft}

We fine-tuned the primary model in three conditions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{PEFT-en} on the 3,803 English training lines, evaluated on English and Croatian;
    \item \textbf{PEFT-hr} on 3,802 of the 3,803 Croatian training lines (one exceeded the 256-token limit), evaluated on Croatian;
    \item \textbf{PEFT-bilingual} on both (7,605 lines), evaluated on English and Croatian.
\end{enumerate}

Each example is a three-message conversation, and the cross-entropy loss is computed on the tokens of the assistant's answer only, with the system and user messages serving as context. We used LoRA, in which an adapted layer computes
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{h} = W_0 \mathbf{x} + \frac{\alpha}{r} B A \mathbf{x},
    \label{eq:lora}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{k}$ is the layer's input and $\mathbf{h} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ its output, $W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$ the frozen pretrained weight matrix, $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ and $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}$ the trained adapter matrices of rank $r \ll \min(d, k)$, and $\alpha$ a scaling constant. $B$ is initialised to zero, so training starts from the unchanged model. With QLoRA, $W_0$ is stored in the 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4) format with double quantisation and converted back to bf16 only when each layer is computed.

All three conditions shared one configuration: QLoRA with adapters on all linear layers, rank $r = 16$, $\alpha = 32$ and dropout $0.05$, giving 50.9M trainable parameters (0.55\% of the model). We fixed the rank, $\alpha$ and dropout rather than tuning them, as the project brief does not require a full hyperparameter grid. We trained with AdamW without weight decay, a learning rate of $1 \times 10^{-4}$ with linear warm-up over the first 3\% of steps and linear decay after, a batch size of 8 with two gradient-accumulation steps (an effective batch of 16), a maximum sequence length of 256 tokens, at most three epochs, and seed 42, with gradient checkpointing to save memory and deterministic training to make runs exactly repeatable. The 256-token limit fits every training example except the one Croatian line (259 tokens). Since validation loss does not measure whether the generated label set is correct, we selected checkpoints by development micro-F1: twice per epoch, the model generated answers for every development line in its training language(s), and we kept the best checkpoint and stopped after two evaluations without improvement. The kept checkpoints are from epoch 1.5 for PEFT-en, 3.0 for PEFT-hr and 0.5 for PEFT-bilingual.

This configuration won a small development-set search over LoRA (bf16) and QLoRA (4-bit) at learning rates of $1$, $2$ and $4 \times 10^{-4}$. Each candidate was trained in all three conditions and scored by its development micro-F1 averaged over the conditions evaluated in each language, then over the two languages, so that Croatian, evaluated in three conditions, does not outweigh English, evaluated in two. All six candidates lay within 0.01 micro-F1 of each other (Table~\ref{tab:selection}). To see how much Croatian training data fine-tuning needs, we also retrained PEFT-hr and PEFT-bilingual with the Croatian training lines capped at 250, 500, 1,000 and 2,000, keeping every other setting unchanged.

\subsection{Prompting}
\label{sec:methodology_prompting}

Both models were prompted zero-shot and five-shot with the same instruction and message format as the fine-tuning data. The five-shot prompt adds five demonstrations as user and assistant turns before the test line, with the same five alignment IDs throughout: English evaluation shows the English lines with their gold labels, and Croatian evaluation the aligned Croatian lines with their projected labels (Table~\ref{tab:demonstrations}). Since few-shot results depend on the demonstrations, we chose them from the training split by a fixed procedure: candidates needed gold English labels, for human-labelled English demonstrations; a direct English--Croatian alignment, making each Croatian demonstration the closest counterpart of its English line; at most 80 characters in both languages, which keeps the prompt short and still admits about 95\% of the lines; and at most three labels, as 98\% of the lines have. From these, we greedily picked the candidate that added the most emotions not yet covered, one per film for variety, breaking ties at random (seed 42).

All answers were decoded greedily, which makes them deterministic, with at most 32 new tokens, enough for a list of all eight labels (31 tokens for EuroLLM-9B and 30 for Qwen3.5-9B). To measure prompt sensitivity, we also scored two reworded instructions and one set of five randomly drawn demonstrations on the development set only; only the default prompt was run on test.

\subsection{Output Parsing}
\label{sec:methodology_parsing}

A single parser trims the surrounding whitespace of every answer and decodes it as JSON; an answer that does not decode to a list is counted as malformed. From a list, it keeps the string items, lowercases them and collapses their whitespace, then drops duplicates and anything that is not one of the eight permitted labels, mapping no synonyms. Malformed answers and answers with no permitted label both count as an empty prediction, and the malformed rate is reported separately. The label set is then binarised into an eight-dimensional vector in the fixed label order.
