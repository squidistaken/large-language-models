\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We compared prompting and parameter-efficient fine-tuning for multi-label emotion classification of English and Croatian subtitles from XED, using EuroLLM-9B for all conditions and Qwen3.5-9B for prompting only. With the same model, fine-tuning clearly outperformed prompting in English, raising micro-F1 from 0.428 with zero-shot prompting to 0.517, and in Croatian the best result came from bilingual fine-tuning (0.436 against 0.380). Much of this gain came from learning how many emotions to report rather than which ones: prompting gave too many labels, especially with five demonstrations, while fine-tuning brought the number of labels close to that of the reference.

Fine-tuning on English alone did not transfer to Croatian. There, it scored no better than zero-shot prompting, carrying over a habit of giving fewer labels but not its better recognition of the emotions. Croatian training data helped, and combining it with English helped most: bilingual fine-tuning scored above Croatian-only fine-tuning at every amount of Croatian data, and with 1,000 Croatian lines it came close to its full-data score. For a mid-low resource language such as Croatian, a modest amount of target-language data added to English data therefore appears to be the most effective use of fine-tuning.

Prompt wording mattered little next to the differences between systems, although Croatian was more sensitive to it than English, and EuroLLM-9B depended more on the choice of demonstrations than on the wording. A stronger base model also narrows the trade-off between the two approaches: five-shot Qwen3.5-9B matched the fine-tuned EuroLLM-9B averaged over three seeds without any training, although it ran at a higher precision. Finally, all Croatian results measure agreement with projected labels. Our error analysis found reference labels inherited from misaligned lines, and our design cannot separate the models' command of Croatian from this noise.

For future explorations, we would first have a Croatian reader verify the test labels, which would measure the projection noise directly and separate model errors from label errors. Beyond this, we see the following directions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Translate-test evaluation:} Machine-translating the Croatian test lines into English and classifying them with PEFT-en would show where the lower Croatian scores come from. If the scores approach those on English, the gap lies in the model's command of Croatian, and if not, in the labels. This is the question our design leaves open.
    \item \textbf{Filtering misaligned pairs:} Scoring each English--Croatian pair with a multilingual sentence-embedding model and rescoring every system on the well-aligned pairs would estimate the projection noise without a Croatian reader, as our error analysis found aligned lines that are not translations of each other.
    \item \textbf{Score-based decoding:} Reading the model's probability for each label and applying a threshold per emotion, instead of generating a free-form list, would control the number of labels directly. Since much of the difference between systems lay in how many labels they gave, and disgust was predicted far too often, this could improve both approaches, and it would also remove answers with labels outside the permitted set.
    \item \textbf{Demonstrations with fewer labels:} Our demonstrations carry about two emotions per line on average, against about 1.45 in the data. Selecting demonstrations that match this distribution would test whether they cause the over-labelling of five-shot prompting, as our prompt-sensitivity results suggest.
    \item \textbf{Dialogue context:} Adding the preceding subtitle lines to the input would address lines that support several readings on their own, which limits how closely any system can agree with a single reference.
    \item \textbf{Fine-tuning the second model:} Five-shot Qwen3.5-9B matched the fine-tuned EuroLLM-9B, but at a higher precision. Fine-tuning both models at the same precision would show, in a controlled comparison, whether fine-tuning still pays off on a stronger base model.
\end{itemize}

Comparing our results with those for other resource bands would, in addition, show whether the gap between fine-tuning and prompting depends on the amount of target-language data.
